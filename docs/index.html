Which pages will fall behind next month?

Fasih · October 2026 · code and notebooks

Abstract

FlyRank's product helps content teams decide which pages to refresh, using hand-tuned rules such as a health score. In practice a team usually notices a falling page only after a monthly report shows the loss. I asked whether signals available on decision day can tell a reviewer which pages to open first. Using the pseudonymised FlyRank search warehouse (about 219,935 page-month rows across up to 37 clients, March to June 2026), I defined the outcome as a page falling more than 20% further than its own site in the following month, after finding that a plain "impressions dropped 20%" label mostly measured whole sites losing traffic. A gradient-boosting ranker built on 18 decision-day signals was compared with a transparent five-sign rule, using a split that is both client-grouped and time-forward, with June 2026 kept sealed until the end. On June and on clients it never saw, the model ordered pages with ROC-AUC 0.702 against 0.602 for the rule, and each site's top-10 flags were right 0.89 of the time against 0.75 for the rule and 0.52 by chance. The output is a per-site review queue with reason codes: decision support for where to look, not a claim about what fixing a page will do.

1 · The problem

This comes straight from FlyRank's refresh workflow. The product already surfaces pages to fix with hand-written scores (health score, priority score, refresh flags), and the internship release deliberately leaves those out so the signal has to be found in raw search data. Picture a reviewer responsible for a few hundred pages on one site. Every month some of those pages lose search visibility. By the time a dashboard shows the drop, the traffic is already gone, and there's no time to read every page every month. The question this project answers is narrow on purpose: which pages on this site should I open first?

A wrong answer costs in two ways. A false alarm wastes a review slot. A miss lets a page keep sliding for weeks. Because reviewers work down a short list, I care most about how good the top of each site's list is, not about getting every page right.

2 · Data

The FlyRank internship warehouse (build v20260703) is a pseudonymised export of daily Google Search Console data: 78.8 million page-day rows across 104 client accounts (55 of them active in March 2026), running to the end of June 2026. I read it in place with DuckDB and only pulled per-page aggregates into memory.

I built four snapshots, on the last day of March, April, May and June 2026. For each one, features come from the 90 days before, and the outcome from the 30 days after. A page enters the population if it had at least 200 impressions in the prior 60 days, is at least 90 days old, and belongs to a client whose Search Console feed covers the whole feature window. That leaves roughly 63,000 to 85,000 pages per snapshot from 25 to 37 clients.

Excluded on purpose: the query-level table (its fixed window overlaps the outcome months), the content "last updated" fields (they are stamped at export time, so they carry future information), GA4 engagement and AI-referral sessions (too little history and too sparse), and every identifier as a feature. Nothing on this page can identify a client, a domain, a URL or a query.

3 · Methodology

The label, and why it changed

My first label was the obvious one: did the page's impressions fall more than 20% next month? Its rate climbed from 52% to 64% to 75% across three months, because whole sites were shrinking at the same time. The median site kept only 74% and then 64% of its prior traffic. A refresh can't fix an algorithm update or a tracking change that hits a whole site, so that label was mostly measuring the wrong thing.

The final label compares each page with its own site: declined = the page's next-month impressions, relative to its own prior 60-day average, came in more than 20% below the same ratio for its whole site. That rate stays flat at about 50% every month.

Signals, baseline, model

Features (18): impressions in three 30-day blocks, two momentum ratios, clicks and CTR, impression-weighted average position with its change and daily volatility, days visible, the share of last month carried by its single biggest day, weekend share, page age, word count and keyword volume (with missing-data flags).

Baseline: a hand-written rule with one point per warning sign: impressions sliding, position slipping, a one-day spike, age over 270 days (the "decay cliff" in FlyRank's March 2026 report), and patchy visibility. It was written before any model and kept frozen.

Model: logistic regression, random forest and histogram gradient boosting were compared on the development month. Gradient boosting won on the metrics chosen in advance (top-of-queue precision) and was frozen before June was opened.

Validation

Every score in this paper comes from a model that has never seen the client it scores, nor the month it is judged on. Clients are hashed into four fixed folds, and each fold is scored by a model trained only on the other folds' earlier snapshots. Development used March → April; the sealed test trained on March and April and was judged on June outcomes, once. Leakage checks included adding an outcome-month feature (the score jumps, so the harness works), the export-time edit flag (excluded), the query-table overlap (excluded) and a population filter that uses no outcome information.

5 · Limitations

  • Observational, not causal. Nothing here shows that reviewing or refreshing a flagged page changes what happens to it. That would need a controlled before/after design.
  • One portfolio, one season. Between 25 and 37 clients with enough history, March to June 2026. Other sites, or a big search-wide change, may behave differently.
  • Site-relative by design. A page that falls exactly with its site counts as "fine". Site-wide drops need a site-level response, not a page queue.
  • A ranking, not a probability. The scores order pages well but aren't calibrated (mean score 0.47 against an actual rate of 0.52 on June), so the confidence tiers are percentiles.
  • Uneven across clients. Per-client AUC on June runs from 0.54 to 0.86 (median 0.71). The queue beats chance for every client, but helps some much more than others.
  • Not Google's algorithm. These are associations in one dataset, not ranking factors.

6 · Ranked recommendations

The live queue scores 87664 pages across 39 sites for July 2026. Across every site's top 10 the suggested actions split as: review 166, review now 102, monitor 92.

  1. Each site works down its own top 10, in order, and reads the reason codes first.
  2. One-day spike: monitor before editing. They usually do fall next month, but that's the spike unwinding, not the page failing.
  3. Impressions and rank both slipping: review now. Compare with what currently ranks for the main query, then refresh content and internal links.
  4. Don't choose refresh targets by age alone. Older pages with real traffic were the steadier ones.
  5. Never auto-delete, redirect or rewrite because a score is high. A person checks for spikes, seasonality and consolidation into a sibling page first.
